Full description

Ask a tea-stall owner how much Thums Up is left and he'll squint at the fridge and guess. Usually he's close. “Usually close” is how a shop runs out of its best seller on a Saturday evening, and how a crate the supplier pushed on him sits unsold for a month.

Every inventory app ever built starts the same way: enter your items, enter your prices, enter your stock. Nobody running a shop with one pair of hands and a queue at the counter has done that twice. It isn't laziness — data entry is a second job that pays nothing.

Ek Photo Dukaan asks for photographs instead.

Photo one: the rate card already taped to the wall. A vision model reads every item and price off it. That's the shop's catalogue, built in thirty seconds.

Photo two: a supplier's invoice — the ones that already arrive as WhatsApp images. The model reads who supplied it, what came in, at what unit cost, and how often. That sets the supplier up as a payable inside Paytm, with a normal order size and a normal amount.

That is the entire setup. Nothing typed.

From there, the payments the shop already takes do the running. A ₹40 payment where chai is ₹10 and a samosa is ₹15 isn't just ₹40 — it's a basket. Thousands of those amounts become a live picture of what's actually selling. The invoices say what came in, the payments say what went out, and the two have to reconcile, because you can't sell what you never bought. Each one sharpens the other.

Then, before the fridge is empty: “Thums Up runs out Thursday afternoon. Order 4 crates from Sharma Traders?” One tap sends the WhatsApp order and queues the Paytm payment to the supplier from photo two.

Two photographs at the start. After that the shop keeps its own books, predicts its own shortages, and orders its own stock — and the owner never counts, scans, or types anything.

The join. Point the camera at a shop and it configures both ends of the inventory loop:

  1. Rate-card photo → catalogue (SKU + price). Missing bootstrap for inference.
  2. Supplier-invoice photo → stock IN + unit cost + Paytm payable. Hard prior that makes amounts identifiable.
  3. Paytm payment-amount stream → stock OUT.
  4. IN and OUT constrain each other. You cannot sell what you never bought.

Without the camera, inference has no catalogue. Without the payment stream, the camera is OCR with nowhere to act. Without Paytm, there is no input and no output.

3. Track and Paytm fit

Track: Theme 2 — AI for Small Businesses. Lives inside Paytm for Business.

The theme PDF requires the solution to extend products a merchant already uses, not stand alone. Sensors are Paytm transaction amounts and Paytm vendor payouts. The action is a Paytm payment to an existing supplier. WhatsApp is the channel where invoices and reorders already happen.

Swap Paytm for another PSP and both sensors and the reorder action disappear. That is the test.

4. Human story

Ramesh runs a tea stall. Lunch rush, one pair of hands, a rate card taped to the wall, Sharma Traders sending invoice photos on WhatsApp. He never counted stock because counting is a second job. On Saturday evening the Thums Up is gone and he finds out from a customer. On Monday a crate of something nobody wanted is still sitting there.

He does not need a dashboard. He needs the shop to notice before he does.

6. Why AI, Paytm, and WhatsApp

AI. Two jobs with no rule-based substitute. (1) Vision on unstandardised, often bilingual rate cards and invoices. (2) Inverse inference: a payment amount is a basket fingerprint. Mixture decomposition plus Bayesian stock, constrained by invoice inflows. Do not pitch a chatbot. Do not let an LLM authorise money. Merchant taps Approve. Paytm executes.

Paytm. Sale amounts, supplier payouts, and the reorder payment all live on Paytm rails. A standalone inventory app would ask Ramesh to type sales — the product we refuse to build.

WhatsApp. Invoices already arrive as photos; reorders already happen as messages. Do not claim native WhatsApp Payments. Send a Paytm payment link or queued Paytm payout. Instagram is not in the MVP.

7. Scope we say out loud

  • One vertical: tea stall / QSR counter.
  • 10–15 items with distinct prices. Overlapping prices break identifiability.
  • Printed rate card only. Handwriting is a demo killer.
  • Cash is a known blind spot: Paytm sales are observed; cash is a prior.
  • A 2,000-SKU kirana is out of scope. Volunteer this before a judge finds it.
“I haven't seen this exact approach: two photos of paper the shop already has, then Paytm payments become the stock register.”

Zero data entry is the differentiator — not OCR, not a forecast chart. If a judge files this as “they OCR'd a price list,” the framing failed.

9. MVP

Must: Paytm Business-like mobile shell; camera/upload rate card → catalogue; invoice → stock IN + supplier; seeded payment stream; decomposition over 10–15 items; stock as a range; stockout card with WhatsApp draft + Paytm queue; demo mode with cached vision and reset.

Do not: Consumer BBPS bills; 2,000-SKU kirana; live handwriting as the judged path; chatbot; native WhatsApp Payments claim; fake learning curves; GST/loans/ads; mixing QR Rakshak into this demo.

11. Judge answers

Isn't this just POS? A POS requires ringing up every item. That is the labour this shop refused. We use the amount he already collected.

Isn't this just OCR? OCR without Paytm is a party trick. The photo creates a catalogue and a payable the payment stream can run against.

Why not a kirana? Overlapping prices and thousands of SKUs make the amount unreadable. We scoped to a menu where prices identify baskets — a product choice, not a hidden shortcut.

Cash? Paytm sales are observed. Cash is a prior. We do not pretend otherwise.

Did the model learn today? No. We have the label and the constraint. Volume trains later. Do not fake a learning curve.
